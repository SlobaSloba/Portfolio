Policy Summary

Bottom line: Gaining access to a work-from-home option did not improve - and in some specifications significantly worsened - the mental health of Dutch employees relative to workers in sectors where remote work was not feasible. Effects are concentrated among parents and full-time employees, consistent with boundary-blurring and work–family conflict mechanisms. Simply expanding WFH access is not sufficient to improve worker wellbeing; the quality of the arrangement matters.

Context and Research Question

The COVID-19 pandemic triggered a sudden and large-scale shift to remote work across the Dutch labour market. By 2022, hybrid working had become a permanent feature of many sectors - raising a direct question for labour market policy: did gaining access to a work-from-home option actually improve employees' mental health?

This is not obvious on theoretical grounds. More autonomy and no commute argue in favour; blurred work–life boundaries, reduced social contact, and work–family conflict argue against. This project provides causal evidence on the net effect using longitudinal data that tracks the same Dutch workers before and after the pandemic-driven WFH expansion.

Mental health is measured using the MHI-5 - a validated 5-item instrument yielding a 0–100 scale, where scores of 60 or above classify a respondent as mentally healthy (CBS, 2015). The treatment variable is a binary indicator for having the option to work from home, drawn from the LISS Work and Schooling module.

Identification Strategy

The core challenge is that workers in WFH-capable jobs are not randomly selected - they tend to work in different sectors, have different educational backgrounds, and may differ in baseline mental health. A simple before/after or treated/untreated comparison would therefore be confounded.

The solution is a Difference-in-Differences (DiD) design that exploits the COVID-19 pandemic as an exogenous shock to WFH feasibility. The identifying variation comes from cross-sector differences in teleworkability (following Dingel & Neiman, 2020): sectors where WFH became feasible form the treatment group; sectors where it did not (healthcare, manufacturing, retail, construction) form the control group.

The preferred specification is a two-way fixed effects (TWFE) estimator:

MHI_it = α + β (Treat_i × Post_t) + γ_i + δ_t + X_it'Γ + ε_it
  • γ_i - individual fixed effects: absorb all time-invariant confounders (personality, baseline health, occupation type)
  • δ_t - year fixed effects: control for economy-wide shocks common to all workers
  • β - the coefficient of interest: the average treatment effect on the treated
  • X_it - time-varying controls: household size (aantalki), age category (lftdcat)

Standard errors are clustered at the individual level. Wild-cluster bootstrap inference (9,999 replications, sector-clustered, via boottest) is applied in the preferred specification to address the small-number-of-clusters problem.

Why COVID years are excluded

The years 2020 and 2021 are excluded from the main analysis. During this period, the Dutch government mandated WFH under strict lockdown and curfew rules - including a hard lockdown in December 2021 with the Omicron wave. This creates a mechanical correlation between WFH status and the mental health effects of the lockdown itself, making clean identification impossible. The economy only fully reopened in early 2022. Including these years yields insignificant coefficients, consistent with the pandemic's confounding effect washing out the treatment signal.

Parallel Trends Validation

The DiD estimator requires that, absent the treatment, the treatment and control groups would have followed the same MHI-5 trajectory - the parallel trends assumption. The figures below plot mean MHI-5 by year for each group with 95% confidence intervals.

The pre-treatment period (2019–2020) shows near-parallel trends for both groups, supporting the identifying assumption. The post-treatment divergence - with the treatment group declining relative to the control - is the opposite of what a positive WFH effect would predict, motivating the formal estimation below.

Code Walkthrough

Data preparation (R)

library(tidyverse)
library(haven)

# Load and merge LISS core modules (Background, Health, Work & Schooling)
bg   <- read_dta("background_2019_2024.dta")
hlth <- read_dta("health_2019_2024.dta")
work <- read_dta("work_schooling_2019_2024.dta")

panel <- bg |>
  left_join(hlth, by = c("nomem_encr", "wave_year")) |>
  left_join(work, by = c("nomem_encr", "wave_year"))

# Keep employed / self-employed / family business only
panel <- panel |> filter(belbezig %in% c(1, 2, 3))

# Exclude COVID years
panel <- panel |> filter(!wave_year %in% c(2020, 2021))

# Construct MHI-5 (positive items scored 5-0, negative items reversed, ×4)
panel <- panel |>
  mutate(MHI = (mhi_anxious_r + mhi_down_r + mhi_calm +
                mhi_depressed_r + mhi_happy) * 4)

# Binary WFH option treatment
panel <- panel |> mutate(treat = as.integer(wfh_option == 1))

# Sector classification: high vs low WFH uptake
high_wfh <- c("Finance", "IT", "Professional Services",
              "Education", "Public Administration")
panel <- panel |>
  mutate(treat_group = as.integer(sector %in% high_wfh))

# Drop sector switchers (ensures clean treatment assignment)
switchers <- panel |>
  group_by(nomem_encr) |>
  summarise(n_sec = n_distinct(sector)) |>
  filter(n_sec > 1) |> pull(nomem_encr)
panel <- panel |> filter(!nomem_encr %in% switchers)

write_dta(panel, "liss_panel_clean.dta")

DiD estimation (Stata)

xtset nomem_encr wave_year
gen post       = (wave_year >= 2022)
gen treat_post = treat_group * post

* Baseline DiD (no fixed effects)
reg MHI treat_group post treat_post, vce(cluster nomem_encr)
estimates store m1_baseline

* TWFE: individual + year fixed effects
areg MHI treat_post i.wave_year, absorb(nomem_encr) vce(cluster nomem_encr)
estimates store m2_twfe

* TWFE + controls + wild-cluster bootstrap (preferred)
areg MHI treat_post i.wave_year aantalki lftdcat, ///
     absorb(nomem_encr) cluster(sector)
boottest treat_post, bootcluster(sector) reps(9999) seed(12345)
estimates store m3_preferred

* Placebo test: 2019–2020 as fake post-period (coeff should ≈ 0)
preserve
  keep if wave_year <= 2020
  gen post_p = (wave_year == 2020)
  gen tp_p   = treat_group * post_p
  areg MHI tp_p i.wave_year, absorb(nomem_encr) cluster(sector)
restore

* Balanced subsample robustness check (2019 + 2022 only)
preserve
  keep if inlist(wave_year, 2019, 2022)
  gen post2 = (wave_year == 2022)
  gen tp2   = treat_group * post2
  areg MHI tp2 i.wave_year aantalki lftdcat, ///
       absorb(nomem_encr) cluster(sector)
  boottest tp2, bootcluster(sector) reps(9999) seed(12345)
restore

Results

Table 1 summarises all estimated DiD coefficients. With COVID years included, the coefficients are −0.776 (baseline) and −0.587 (controlled) - both negative, neither statistically significant. This is expected: the mandatory pandemic-era WFH and the lockdown's direct mental health effects are inseparable in that window, yielding a noisy estimate.

With COVID years excluded, the baseline coefficient is −1.047, significant at the 10% level. Across all specifications, the point estimate is consistently negative, indicating that workers who gained the WFH option experienced a relative decline in MHI-5 compared to workers in non-teleworkable sectors.

Heterogeneity analysis confirms that the negative effects are concentrated among parents and full-time employees - consistent with the theoretical prediction that boundary blurring and work–family conflict are the dominant channels, rather than autonomy gains.

Policy Recommendations

Caution on WFH mandates and blanket policies. Expanding WFH access uniformly across the workforce is unlikely to yield mental health benefits, and may harm specific groups - particularly workers with caregiving responsibilities and those in full-time roles with limited ability to set boundaries.

The findings point to three concrete directions for policy and employer practice:

1. Prioritise arrangement quality over access alone. The mental health effect of WFH depends on whether workers can maintain clear work–life boundaries, access managerial support, and maintain social connection with colleagues. Policies should focus on these enabling conditions - not just on whether staff technically have a WFH option.

2. Design hybrid-first rather than remote-first arrangements. Evidence from the broader literature, and the heterogeneity patterns in this study, suggest that part-time WFH combined with regular in-person interaction is more conducive to wellbeing than full-time home working. Employers introducing formal hybrid policies should set minimum in-office requirements for social cohesion.

3. Target support at high-risk subgroups. Workers with children and full-time employees show the largest negative effects. Employers should consider additional support for these groups - for example flexible scheduling, childcare provisions, or explicit "right to disconnect" policies - when extending WFH options.

Data: LISS Panel (Longitudinal Internet Studies for the Social Sciences), waves 2019–2024.
Analysis: R (data preparation) and Stata (estimation). DiD with TWFE, individual and year fixed effects, wild-cluster bootstrap inference.
Outcome measure: MHI-5, scaled 0–100 (CBS, 2015).
